Evaluating a Retrieval System

Why generation quality is the wrong first thing to measure

When a retrieval augmented system answers badly, the cause is almost always retrieval
rather than generation. A modern language model asked to answer from three relevant
passages will do so competently. The same model asked to answer from three irrelevant
passages will produce something fluent and wrong. Measuring answer quality therefore
measures retrieval quality with extra noise and expense layered on top. Retrieval
should be measured directly and first.

Recall at k

Recall@k asks whether the correct passage appears anywhere in the top k results. It
is the single most useful retrieval metric for this kind of system, because the
generator sees all k passages and does not care which position the right one occupied.
Recall@5 of 0.8 means that for eight questions in ten, the answer was somewhere in
the context handed to the model.

Precision matters far less here than in classical search. Two irrelevant passages
alongside three relevant ones rarely damage an answer, provided the model has been
instructed to answer only from what it was given.

Mean reciprocal rank

MRR rewards putting the right passage first. It is the right metric when only the top
result is shown to a user, and the wrong metric when the top five are all passed to a
model. Reporting MRR for a system that consumes five passages describes a property
the system does not depend on.

Latency percentiles

Report latency as percentiles, never as a mean. Retrieval latency distributions have
a long right tail: a cold index, a page fault, or a garbage collection pause produces
occasional requests an order of magnitude slower than typical. A mean blends those
into a number that describes no actual request. The p50 describes the common case,
the p95 and p99 describe what a user notices and complains about.

The similarity score as a diagnostic

The top-1 similarity score is the cheapest early warning available. When the best
passage in the collection scores poorly against a question, no amount of prompt
engineering will rescue the answer, because the necessary information is not in the
context. Recording that score with every query turns "the answer was wrong" into
"the answer was wrong and the best available passage scored 0.19", which points at
the corpus rather than the prompt.

Setting a similarity floor

A floor discards results too weak to be worth sending to the model. It must be set
from measurement. Run a set of questions known to be answerable by the corpus and a
set known not to be, record the top-1 similarity of each, and look at where the two
distributions separate. In practice on this corpus, answerable questions cluster
above 0.30 and unanswerable ones fall below 0.15, so a floor of 0.25 separates them
with margin on both sides.

A floor that is too high refuses questions the corpus can actually answer. A floor
that is too low lets the model improvise over noise. The refusal rate -- the share of
queries where nothing cleared the floor -- should be tracked, because a sudden rise
in it usually means the corpus changed rather than the code.

Building a question set

Fifteen to twenty questions written against documents you actually have is worth more
than a thousand synthetic ones. Write them by reading the corpus and asking what a
real user would want. Record the expected answer and the page it came from, so recall
can be computed automatically rather than judged by eye each time.

Deliberately include questions designed to fail. Vocabulary mismatch questions use
synonyms absent from the source text. Boundary questions target facts that straddle a
chunk boundary. Multi-hop questions need two facts from distant sections. A question
set containing only questions the system answers correctly measures nothing.
